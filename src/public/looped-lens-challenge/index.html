The challenge

A lens reads an activation from inside a model and says, in tokens, what the model is computing at that point. The logit lens and its relatives all read the same thing: the residual stream, the running sum that every layer of an ordinary transformer adds to.

Inkling 2 Mini is a small open-weights model from Thinking Machines. It loops its middle layers, so it does more computation on each token instead of writing more tokens. It also has no single residual stream: each layer reads its own mix of what earlier layers wrote. So what happens to the lenses we use to understand and monitor a model? Does a Jacobian lens still work the same way?

This challenge asks the community to find out: build the lens that best reads Inkling 2 Mini. It runs on NDIF, which hosts the model, and it is open to anyone with an NDIF key. You write an experiment on your own machine with nnsight, and NDIF runs it on the model.

Inside Inkling 2 Mini

Inkling 2 Mini has 24 layers of weights and applies 48 to every token. The middle twelve run three times, with the same weights on each pass.

The nnsight blog post Opening the box of Inkling 2 Mini explains the loop and the blocks, with code to read them on NDIF.

Why it matters

Looped transformers are getting attention as an architecture that performs well, and as a subject for interpretability. Inkling 2 Mini is a major one: it runs on a laptop, and it is performant enough to power workflows in coding, agentic work, computer use and design.

At the same time, frontier models can do more and more of their reasoning inside a single forward pass, with no chain of thought to read: see the reports from Redwood Research, Epoch AI and the Alignment Forum. As part of this release, Thinking Machines studied the effects of looping on chain-of-thought monitorability for Inkling 2 Mini and is publishing the results, along with a second model built for studying looping.

Reasoning that a model does not write down can only be read from its activations. Lenses do that, and they have had little testing on looped models.

The science of lenses

One of the grand challenges in our field of AI interpretability is building good lenses: at some layer in the model, how do we know what concepts or “thoughts” the model is considering? This is challenging both methodologically (what are the right subspaces and transformations for the best lens?) and from a ground-truth perspective. How can we ever know what a model is truly thinking? Who should decide what the ground truth here is, and the appropriate benchmark examples to test our lenses?

Since the “first” lens in 2020, the logit lens (and, if you consider linear probes, much earlier), our field has come up with highly sophisticated lenses. Some of them are improvements over the logit lens, such as the tuned lens or the J-lens, while others take very different approaches, such as LatentLens, or even use a second meta-model (NLAs, Activation Oracles), which can sometimes be the same model itself again (Patchscopes). With the popularity of the lens question, we see new lenses pop up every week. Let’s see if this competition can bring forth some better ones! Or some that are particularly suited to Inkling 2 Mini’s looped layers and its lack of a single residual stream.

So how good are these lenses? There are surprisingly few stand-alone, unified benchmarks that measure this; many lens papers instead come with their own evaluation. Recently, WorkspaceBench was proposed by Camila Blank et al., which we use as inspiration for our competition. The overall philosophy of such evaluations is simple: let’s try to find prompts where we can be relatively certain some intermediate concept is needed to get to the right final answer (i.e. “multi-hop” tasks). For example, when a model computes “(3 + 5) × 4” in a single forward pass, it first needs to compute the result of 3 + 5 before outputting the final answer 32. WorkspaceBench applies this idea across 20+ task families. When looking at the overall accuracy of all the lenses, it becomes clear: there is a lot of headroom for improvement!

The competition

The goal is to crowdsource two things: new tools for working on this class of model, and general insights into Inkling 2 Mini itself. You enter by writing a lens. These are the rules.

Submission
A Python script, limited in size, with your lens artifacts sent as files or as links to files. The steps to submit are under Take part.
Input
Your program receives one prompt from our evaluation set.
Read locations
You choose up to 10 of the 48 effective layers to read on each prompt, so picking good layers is part of the task. Readouts are taken at a single token position per prompt. You can choose that too, though it should probably be the final token.
Output
The top 10 ranked tokens at each of up to 10 read locations: at most 10 × 10 tokens for the prompt, labelled with the layer they came from.
Scoring
The readouts for a prompt are collected into one list, and the list is given to an LLM judge.
Increasing complexity
Our initial evaluation set is meant to be easier (e.g., single-token readouts). Throughout the competition we will increase complexity. The initial phase is designed to get participants started with the competition, and consists of single-token readouts on relatively simple tasks in the style of workspace-bench. The final leaderboard will be a mix of the early simpler and later more complex evaluation.
Entry
Anyone can enroll. You need an NDIF key and a registration/waitlist. Due to limited compute capacity, we have a waitlist for later participants.
Dates
Phase one opens on November 23. Its closing date will be announced here.
What makes a valid submission?

This competition is about exploring the new Inkling model using NDIF and pushing the frontier of interpretability techniques; the goal is not to find the best way to game our scoring system. With that in mind, we require that all valid submissions to the Looped Lens challenge represent legitimate attempts to build lenses for Inkling. Examples of invalid submissions include scripts that:

  • Don’t look at activations at all
  • Rely on calls to external models in order to produce readouts (Note: this may be relaxed in future phases to allow for tools like NLAs, activation oracles, etc)
  • Rely on analysis of the input prompt itself to construct readouts
  • Etc

All winning submissions will be audited, and any that do not fit the spirit of the competition will be disqualified.

Competition updates
Both the model and the systems being used to evaluate lenses in this competition are new, so we fully expect there to be a learning curve on our end in terms of best to organize the Looped Lens challenge. We therefore reserve the right to alter the backend evaluation system as the competition progresses, including changing the eval datasets, judge prompts, and scoring code. Competition scores and rankings may shift as a result of these changes.

TLDR, a submission workflow looks like this:

  1. Get an API key at login.ndif.us.
  2. Register for the challenge with the registration/waitlist form. Use the email your NDIF key was created with.
  3. Clone the repository and install it with uv.
    git clone https://github.com/ndif-team/looped-lens-challenge
cd looped-lens-challenge
uv sync && source .venv/bin/activate
export NDIF_API_KEY=...
  4. Run the starter logit lens on the 10 practice questions. It executes on NDIF and is scored on your machine by exact matching. To score with the leaderboard's judge instead, run uv sync --group evaluator and set OPENAI_API_KEY.
    python submit.py --local
  5. Write your lens in submission/lenses.py. For each question it reads up to 10 of the model's 48 effective layers and returns 10 tokens from each. Trained weights go on the Hugging Face Hub and are loaded with lenses.fetch.
  6. Submit. The team name is needed the first time only, and one NDIF account is one team. Your best score appears on the leaderboard.
    python submit.py --team "My Team" --wait
python submit.py --status

The repository's README has the exact output format, the limits and the scoring.

Rule clarifications

November 24. The first submissions in the last 24h interpret our rules in different ways. Since our scoring code does not check them, we clarify the rules here in more detail. Note: we don't add any new rules; each point restates a rule above.

One position
All readouts for a prompt are taken at the same single token position. You may choose it from the activations (counting any visit you read to decide, see below), or use the prompt's punctuation or chat template to find it (for example, the last question mark). You may not choose it by comparing what readouts at several candidate positions say.
One visit per readout
A prompt reads at most 10 of the 48 layer visits, counting visits used only to make decisions. Each readout is the top 10 of one lens applied to the state at one visit, and is labelled with that visit's number, 0 to 47. No visit appears twice. A lens may run the model's own later layers on that state, as a coda lens does; the model's own attention then sees the other positions, which is fine.
No prompt text
Readouts may not be built, filtered or reranked using the prompt's text or token ids.
Allowed
  • Logit, tuned, translator and coda lenses on one visit at the chosen position. Example: a tuned lens at ten visits between 20 and 47, all at the last token, each labelled with its visit.
  • An ensemble of lenses on the same visit and position, combined into one readout. Example: the logit lens and a tuned lens at visit 40, last token, with their probabilities averaged into one top-10 labelled 40.
  • Different visits, or a different position, for each prompt. Example: visits 20–29 for one prompt and 38–47 for another; the last token for one prompt and the last “?” for another.
  • Cleaning a readout: removing control tokens such as <|end_message|>, and repeats of a token in another casing or spacing (“ Paris” after “Paris”).
Not allowed
  • More than one readout from the same visit (each visit gives at most one top-10 list, whichever lenses produce it), or labels that are not visit numbers. Example: a logit-lens readout labelled 24 and a tuned-lens readout labelled "24b".
  • Readouts that combine token positions. Examples: scoring each token by its best probability over the last 8 tokens; alternating the top tokens of two positions; subtracting the state at another position.
  • Readouts that combine visits. Examples: one top-10 from a weighted sum of logit lenses at visits 4–47, labelled 47; subtracting visit 12's logits from visit 40's.
  • Reading many more readouts than you submit and keeping the ones that agree. Example: reading 13 visits at every token of the prompt, a few hundred readouts, then submitting six whose top word is the same plus four others, so that this word wins the vote. Or: reading ten visits at two candidate positions and submitting the set whose readouts agree more.
  • Dropping or demoting readout tokens because they appear in the prompt. Example: for “What is 9 − 2?”, removing “9” and “2” from readouts, or ranking them lower.
What happens now
Submissions that do not follow these rules will be removed from the leaderboard, to be fair to everyone, and winning submissions will be audited against them. This applies to individual submissions, not to teams: a team whose submission is removed is still in the competition and can resubmit a lens that follows the rules. Not sure whether your lens qualifies? Ask on Discord.
